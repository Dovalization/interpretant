# Devlog 05 — Who Predicts Whom, a Wrong Reading, and the Corpus Lemmatised

*November 2026*

---

Devlog 04 ended with a list. Two of its items are done: cross-perplexity between cells, to
measure who borrows from whom, and a rebuild of the corpus with plurals and spellings
collapsed. Both were run the same way as everything since the redesign, with predictions
committed to git before the evidence existed, and both produced results I would not have
written down in advance. This devlog is about those two, and about what the ledger of
predictions looks like after eight rounds.

---

## Who predicts whom

Word vectors answer one kind of question: how a word's company changed. They do not say
whether one field's *language* became more like another's. For that I used an older and
simpler instrument: a bigram language model with Kneser-Ney smoothing, one per field and
decade, each scored on every other field's held-out abstracts. The number that matters is the
**excess cross-entropy** E(A → B): the bits per token that B's held-out text costs under A's
model, over what it costs under B's own. Low E(A → B) means B's language is predictable from
A's. Vilhena and colleagues used the same quantity in 2014 to find "cultural holes" between
scientific communities.

The first run looked good: five of six predictions passed. Then, reading the numbers, I found
three design flaws I should have seen before running anything. OpenAlex files AI under
computer science and cognitive psychology under psychology, so a fifth of AI's abstracts were
also in computer science's cell, and a parent's model could have trained on the subfield's
held-out text. Training size was equal within a decade but not across decades, so a comparison
of the 1980s with the 2010s compared a model trained on 200,000 tokens with one trained on a
million. And there was no uncertainty estimate at all; one of the passes was a change of 0.05
bits that could have been sampling noise.

The corrected run (shared abstracts removed from every cell of a decade, exactly 200,000
training tokens everywhere, five seeds each drawing its own held-out set) was committed as new
predictions, not as edits to the old ones. Three passed, three failed, and two of the failures
went the opposite way from what I had predicted:

- **The specialisation of fields was a training-size artefact.** With equal training, the mean
  excess between fields *fell* from the 1980s to the 2010s in all five seeds. Fields converged.
- **Parents do not predict their subfields better than the reverse.** Without shared
  abstracts, AI's model predicts computer science's text better than computer science's
  predicts AI's, in every decade, and the same for cognitive psychology and psychology.

What did hold, in all five seeds, was the result I cared most about: E(philosophy → HCI) fell
from the 1990s to the 2020s. I wrote it up as "HCI's language moved towards philosophy's", the
whole-language counterpart of the *embodiment* finding from devlog 04, and committed a
follow-up to pin it down: the move should be concentrated after 2000, one-sided, and carried
by philosophy's words.

Two of the three failed. The movement is one-sided, but it is not concentrated after 2000 (the
two fields were closest in the 2010s and drifted apart again), and the words whose excess fell
most were not philosophy's. They were *user, design, interface, virtual, digital, interaction*:
HCI's own vocabulary. E(A → B) falls when B moves towards A, but also when A broadens to cover
B. The words said the second. So the sentence in the README was wrong and had to be replaced:
**philosophy's language model came to predict HCI's abstracts better, while HCI's model did
not come to predict philosophy's.**

That suggested a new explanation, philosophy turning towards technology, and a new round of
predictions to test it on evidence I had not looked at: philosophy's pairs with computer
science and AI, a technology lexicon in the full philosophy cells that excluded every word of
the exploratory count, and a per-occurrence word ranking. It failed too. Philosophy's model got
*worse* at predicting computer science and AI over the same period; its technology vocabulary
jumps only in the 2020s, while philosophy and HCI were closest in the 2010s. The robust result
stands as measured, it is specific to HCI, and both explanations I could think of are out. Its
cause is open, and I would rather leave it open than publish a third reading without a test.

## The corpus, lemmatised

The neighbour lists in the README figures made a problem visible that the tables had hidden:
*structure* and *structures*, *embedding* and *embeddings*, *conceptualisation* and
*conceptualization* were separate words to the models. That inflates vocabularies, splits
frequencies so that fewer words clear the 150-occurrence threshold, and pads every list of
neighbours with variants of each other. I had collapsed the target words' plurals at
preprocessing, but nothing else.

The obvious fix, a lemmatiser, is the wrong one here. A quick trial of simplemma on the words
that matter turned *learning* into *learn*, *meaning* into *mean*, *embedding* into *embed*,
*embodied* into *embody*, *being* into *be*. The nominalisations are the concepts under study;
a lemmatiser that erases them would destroy the object of the analysis to tidy its vocabulary.
So the map does two things and refuses to do more: regular plurals collapse onto their singular
(simplemma's lemma, accepted only when the token is `lemma + s`, `+ es`, `y → ies` or the
*-is/-es* alternation), and British spellings collapse onto American ones through rules gated
on simplemma's lexicon, an exception list, and the American form's own frequency in the
corpus. The last gate came from the review of the first map, which had happily produced
*controlled → controled* and *surprising → surprizing* because simplemma's lexicon knows forms
nobody writes. Verbs, participles, *-ing* forms, hyphenated and phrase tokens stay as they are.
The map touches 37,000 types and 8.3% of tokens; the review of its 200 most frequent mappings
found nothing wrong after the fix.

Everything downstream was rebuilt: phrases relearned, both TWEC seeds, PPMI-SVD, the drift
tables, cross-perplexity with five seeds. The unlemmatised models moved aside as the frozen
evidence of rounds 1 to 3. Before any of it ran, eleven predictions went into git: the robust
findings should survive, *information*'s convergence (seen in two TWEC seeds, absent in
PPMI-SVD) was stated as a prediction for the first time, the two cross-perplexity reversals
were stated as predictions, and one prediction was about the instrument itself: vocabularies
should shrink by at least 8% in the main grid and the neighbour pools should grow by at least
3%, because merged plurals would lift rare words over the 50-occurrence line.

Nine of eleven passed. *Embodiment*'s two senses held in both seeds (five of the
virtual-reality set in HCI, six of the phenomenological set in philosophy); *intentionality*
stayed put in both; *representation*'s move from symbolic to learned and HCI's experiential
*affordance* held; philosophy's 2020s *consciousness* is a candidate again, and its lemmatised
neighbours carry both of the earlier readings at once, *computational, substrate,
information_processing* beside *qualia, sentience*. The one-sided philosophy → HCI effect, the
convergence of fields and the subfield asymmetry all reproduced within a few thousandths of a
bit.

The two misses are the useful part.

**The instrument prediction was wrong, and wrong about the mechanism.** Vocabularies shrank by
4% to 10%, not the 8%-plus I had set for the main grid, because many plural types had no
singular in the cell to merge into. And the neighbour pools *shrank* by about 12% instead of
growing: when both *structure* and *structures* already clear 50 occurrences, merging them
removes a pool member, and that outweighs the rare words the merge lifts over the line. I had
reasoned from the mapped share of types and not from where the merges happen. The prediction
stays falsified in the ledger, as the first one about the apparatus rather than the data.

***Information*'s convergence is not robust.** Psychology and computer science using
*information* more alike over time had appeared in both unlemmatised TWEC seeds; on the
lemmatised grid the gap is 13 percentile points, under the 15 I had committed to. It joins the
not-robust list. My guess at why is that a type-level vector for *information* averages its
senses, Shannon's and the everyday one, and that the "convergence" was a change in the mixture.
That is a guess, and the sense-level analysis on the roadmap is the test for it.

One small thing the lemmatised corpus surfaced: HCI's 2020s neighbours of *embodiment* now
include *soe*, *soa* and *boi*. They are not noise. They are the field's own abbreviations for
sense of embodiment, sense of agency and body-ownership illusion, and they support the reading
rather than muddying it; the explorer should expand them.

## The ledger after eight rounds

Fifty-six predictions have been scored, all written before their evidence: 33 passed, 23
failed. By round: 3 of 9, 7 of 9, 4 of 8, 5 of 6, 3 of 6, 1 of 3, 1 of 4, 9 of 11. The
pattern across them is the one devlog 04 noticed and this devlog confirms: my guesses are
worse than the method. The two rounds I was most confident about, the first cross-perplexity
run and the follow-up on HCI and philosophy, are the two whose passes meant the least and
whose failures taught the most. Two reversed predictions became confirmed findings once they
were restated and tested on new runs. One published interpretation was wrong and is still in
the file, with the test that killed it underneath.

Alongside the research, the repository grew a static explorer of the same data, so that the
neighbour columns, the drift ranks, the who-predicts-whom matrix and this ledger can be read
without running anything. It is not deployed yet.

## Addendum, the same evening

The decomposition ran before this devlog was a day old. Each of HCI's held-out abstracts was
scored on its own under philosophy's model and under HCI's, in both decades and all five seeds,
against five predictions and a reading rule written first. Three passed and two failed, and
the rule says register, not topics: the whole distribution of HCI abstracts became more
predictable (the median fell as much as the mean), the gain landed on the abstracts with none
of philosophy's theme words while the themed ones barely moved, and the other direction did
not budge.

I then wrote a reading, philosophy acquired HCI's objects without computing's methods, and it
lasted about an hour. A question about whether *the* and *of* should count as words in the
per-word ranking led to a split of the gain into function and content words (function words
were 15% of it), and from there to a decomposition of the fall into a change in HCI's word mix
and a change in philosophy's per-word skill. The mix effect dominates in every seed; the rate
effect is small and changes sign. The words doing the work are HCI's 1990s interface jargon
losing share: *menu, display, graphical, user_interface, software, operator, database*. HCI
stopped writing GUI-engineering prose, and philosophy's model found what replaced it less
foreign. Philosophy learned little; HCI changed, though not towards philosophy's themes, which
is why the other direction never moved. That is the reading now, exploratory and marked as
such in the ledger, with the test that would confirm it named: if HCI's mix is the mechanism,
every field's model should have gained on HCI, not only philosophy's. Those cells are in the
corrected table and nobody has looked at them.

So I pre-registered that test and ran it the same night, on cells of the corrected table nobody
had looked at. Three of four passed. Psychology's, sociology's, neuroscience's, cognitive
psychology's, communication's and linguistics' models all gained on HCI in every seed, and by
more than they gained on other texts; the gain is the word mix (for psychology's model the
per-word rates did not change at all); and the two models that did not gain, the one miss the
first prediction needed, were AI's and computer science's. Philosophy's gain, the result five
rounds had been built on, is among the smallest. HCI's language moved out of computing's
register and into the behavioural and social sciences'; nobody moved towards HCI. The README
now says that, and "philosophy's model learned to predict HCI" is what it always was, one
instance of it, seen first because it was the pair I was looking at.

One thing the runs showed that nobody predicted: the share of HCI abstracts using philosophy's
theme words doubled between the 1990s and the 2020s.

## Where it stands

Two findings are robust across seeds, methods and the lemmatised corpus; three hold in part;
one has been retired; one effect, first seen as philosophy's, turned out to be HCI's. Next:

- sense-level drift with contextual embeddings for *information* and *consciousness*, the two
  words whose type-level vectors mix senses, with the predictions written first;
- dating HCI's move decade by decade, and asking whether other fields made one like it;
- the explorer online, and the engineering debts that a reader of the code would notice
  before a reader of the results would.
