# Devlog 02 — The First Training Run, and Why It Didn't Finish

*March 2026*

---

The last devlog ended with "I should get the first results soon."

That was optimistic.

---

## What happened

I kicked off the TWEC training overnight. The compass phase — where the model trains on all decades' text concatenated — had been running for eleven hours when I checked in the morning. It hadn't finished. CPU sitting at 668%. No visible progress. No estimate of how long was left.

The immediate question was: is this working, or is something wrong?

It was working. That was almost the problem.

The compass was training on every document in every corpus across every decade — PubMed alone is 30.5 million abstracts. TWEC trains a single Word2Vec model on that entire concatenation before touching any decade model. At 10 epochs, with a Python iterator feeding gensim sentence by sentence, that's somewhere north of 300 million sentence passes.

The fix has two parts.

First: cap documents per decade file. PubMed has hundreds of thousands of abstracts per decade — far more than needed for co-occurrence statistics to converge. The books corpus and ACL/arXiv are small enough that no cap is needed in practice; the cap only bites on PubMed, which is where the blowup was happening.

Second: reduce epochs from 10 to 5. The compass is estimating a coordinate system, not fitting a predictive model. 5 epochs is enough for the context vectors to stabilize.

I also added per-epoch progress bars to the training loop — compass and each decade model show a tqdm bar — so next time I can actually see where it is.

---

## The corpus got bigger while I waited

While the first training run was eating CPU, I used the time to fill gaps in the early decades.

The original corpus started at 1890. That was already a fiction: Hegel (1807), Marx (1848/1867), Dostoevsky (1864–1880), and Nietzsche (1883–1887) were all filed under 1890s because the directories didn't exist yet. The window has now been extended to the 1820s — 200 years — and the books moved to their correct decades.

That opened up seven new decade directories needing content. What got added:

**1820s** — Schopenhauer's *World as Will and Representation* (1818) and Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* (1820). Schopenhauer is the first serious treatment of representation as something the nervous system constructs rather than the world simply provides — important pre-history for the concept before cognitive science picks it up.

**1830s** — Tocqueville's *Democracy in America* and Comte's *Course of Positive Philosophy*. Comte coins "sociology" here. The vocabulary of social systems, collective behaviour, and observable structure enters the record.

**1840s** — Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling*, Mill's *System of Logic*, Marx and Engels' *Communist Manifesto*. The manifesto is in the corpus mainly because Marx's vocabulary — alienation, commodity, reproduction — has a longer afterlife than most.

**1850s** — Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*, Mill's *On Liberty*. Darwin is obvious. "Selection", "adaptation", "fitness", "variation" — these words get borrowed into every adjacent field over the following 150 years.

**1860s** — Mill's *Utilitarianism*, Marx's *Capital Vol. 1*, Dostoevsky's *Notes from Underground* and *Crime and Punishment*. The 1860s is also when Brentano is starting to write — his *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874) lands in the 1870s and is probably the most important single addition in this batch. "Intentionality" as a technical philosophical concept originates there, decades before Husserl inherits and systematizes it.

**1870s** — Darwin's *Descent of Man*, Brentano.

**1880s** — Nietzsche's *Birth of Tragedy*, *Gay Science*, *Beyond Good and Evil*, *Genealogy of Morality*, *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*. Five Nietzsche texts across one decade, which is a lot — but his vocabulary of will, power, value, interpretation, and perspective is woven through 20th century continental philosophy so densely that leaving it out would create a real gap.

On top of the early decades, I added several texts that were missing from the later part of the corpus: McLuhan's *Gutenberg Galaxy* (1960s), Wundt's *Principles of Physiological Psychology* (1870s, the founding text of experimental psychology), Tarski's *Introduction to Logic* (1930s), Langer's *Feeling and Form* (1950s), Morris's *Signs, Language and Behavior* (1940s), Galloway's *Protocol* (2000s), and Hansen's *New Philosophy for New Media* (2000s).

Hansen was the most annoying. The PDF is a scanned book — PyMuPDF returned nothing. Docling (the ML-based OCR pipeline in the stack) handled it, but the extraction process took about twenty minutes of CPU before producing 114,985 words at acceptable quality. Worth it: Hansen is one of the few serious theoretical treatments of digital media that doesn't collapse into either pure semiotics or pure interface design.

The corpus now has 240 books across 20 decades, approximately 27 million words total. The 1820s–1950s are still books-only — the scientific corpora don't reach back that far — but the books layer is now dense enough in those decades to establish the philosophical and scientific vocabulary before the academic publishing explosion.

---

## Running again, and again

All of this is running on a MacBook Air M3 with 24 GB unified memory. No GPU, no cluster — the M3's efficiency cores handle the gensim training threads and the machine stays usable throughout, though it runs warm. The MacBook Air is fanless, which means there's no audible feedback that something is happening — just a warm palm rest and Activity Monitor showing 400% CPU.

The 24 GB ceiling is part of why the streaming refactor mattered: loading 30M PubMed abstracts into RAM before training would have hit the limit before the compass saw a single sentence.

I kicked off the second run with a 500,000 document cap per file. That's still ~21 million sentences per epoch. An hour in, still in the compass phase. I checked the actual line counts in the PubMed decade files:

```
1960s:  871,633 abstracts
1970s:  1,648,462
1980s:  2,565,188
1990s:  3,844,637
2000s:  5,814,957
2010s:  9,871,171
2020s:  5,869,696
```

The 2010s PubMed file alone has 9.8 million abstracts. Even capped at 500k per file, PubMed was contributing 3.5 million documents per epoch — at ~150 words per abstract, that's over 500 million word co-occurrence observations per epoch. The compass doesn't need that. Co-occurrence distributions for high-frequency terms converge well before you exhaust a corpus that size.

Killed it again. New cap: 100,000 per file. After one epoch completed in 27 minutes, tqdm projected 1h48 remaining for the compass alone. Still too much.

The cap came down to 20,000. The words of interest are high-frequency across every corpus — they appear thousands of times even in the books layer, which is uncapped. Their co-occurrence distributions are stable well below that threshold. 20k per file is enough.

There was one more problem with capping: the PubMed files are sorted by publication date, so taking the first N lines of a 9.8M-line file produces a biased sample — the "2010s" model would only see papers from 2010–2011. The fix is systematic sampling: count total lines once, compute a sampling interval, draw every kth document. The resulting sample is temporally representative of the full decade.

The fourth run finished the compass phase — five epochs, 42 files, ~30k words/second. The compass saved cleanly at 364MB.

Then it crashed.

---

## The bug that was already there

The crash was a `NameError` on the first decade model. A variable had been renamed during refactoring — `callback` → `freeze_cb` — but one reference in a logger call was missed. The fix was in the source before the process started, but the running process had old bytecode loaded. It got through the entire compass phase and the first decade model before the logger line fired.

The compass was already saved. Rather than retrain it (another ~30 minutes), I added a `--load-compass` option to the CLI so the decade training can resume from a saved checkpoint. Restarted with that flag; the decade models trained in under four minutes.

---

## A correctness bug found while waiting

With the training restarted, I audited the downstream pipeline — specifically `drift compute`, which reads the `.kv` files and computes the metrics.

Two problems:

**First**: words requested via `--words` that don't appear in every decade's vocabulary (for example, "affordance" predates Gibson's 1966 coinage) would crash with a `KeyError` rather than skipping gracefully. Fixed with a warning and filter.

**Second**, and more consequential: when computing nearest-neighbour shift (Jaccard distance between top-25 neighbour sets), the search space was limited to the target words themselves — in our case 30 words. Finding "top-25 neighbours" from a pool of 29 candidates produces a Jaccard distance that measures almost nothing. The fix: build full shared-vocabulary context vectors separately and use those as the neighbour search space, regardless of how many target words are specified.

The metric is called *neighbourhood shift* in Hamilton et al. (2016), who use it alongside cosine distance to distinguish two qualitatively different types of semantic change: a word can drift in vector space while its neighbourhood stays structurally intact, or its neighbourhood can swap out while the vector barely moves. The second pattern is what you'd expect from field transfer — "network" migrating from sociology to machine learning while still being surrounded by plausibly related terms.

---

## Where it stands (after training)

Twenty-one decade models trained, one `.kv` file per decade. The compass and all decade models are in `models/twec/`. Running `drift compute` next.

## The first real drift compute run

Twenty-one decades trained. Time to see if the metrics say anything interesting.

```bash
uv run interpretant drift compute --model-dir models/twec -w network -w affordance -w intentionality
```

Two immediate problems.

**First**: all three words were silently dropped. The shared-vocabulary logic — which required a word to appear in every decade — excluded them entirely. "Affordance" wasn't coined until Gibson's 1966 *Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*; it has no business appearing in an 1820s model. "Intentionality" is Brentano (1874), but the early decades are books-only, and whether it appears in the 1820s model depends on what Schopenhauer and Hegel happened to use. "Network" appears everywhere eventually, but not necessarily in every decade when the early corpus is thin.

The existing warning-and-skip code (fixed in the audit session earlier in this devlog) handled words missing from the *global intersection* — but then the fix was to skip those words entirely, which is the wrong move. The right fix is per-pair presence: compute metrics for each (word, t₀, t₁) transition where the word exists in both decades, and leave the gaps as gaps. A word can have a drift score for 1990→2000 without needing one for 1830→1840.

This required rethinking the decade index. Previously: build one index over the shared global vocabulary, reuse it across all transitions. Now: each decade gets an index over its full vocabulary, and each transition filters to the intersection of those two vocabularies. The `ProcrustesAligner` got two new methods — `vocabulary_for_pair(t0, t1)` and `vectors_for_decade(decade)` — and the CLI loop was restructured around them. `avg_pairwise_distance`, which genuinely does need all decades, is computed separately for globally shared words only and left-joined as a nullable column.

**Second**: the neighbourhood shift metric was producing nearly useless output. With k=25 neighbours drawn from a pool of 5,342 shared words, the mean Jaccard distance across all words was 0.61, and most words clustered between 0.8 and 0.98. In principle, neighbourhood shift should distinguish two qualitatively different kinds of semantic change: a word can drift geometrically (cosine distance) while its neighbours stay structurally intact, or its neighbourhood can rotate out from under it while the vector barely moves. The first pattern suggests gradual drift; the second suggests field transfer — a concept being adopted by a new discipline with a different set of surrounding terms.

At k=25 over 5,342 words, the denominator of the Jaccard coefficient (union of the two top-25 sets) grows large enough that any non-trivial movement produces near-total turnover. The metric was detecting noise, not signal. Lowering k to 10 tightens the neighbourhood to the genuinely closest terms — the ones a word is most reliably associated with — and makes the metric sensitive to real shifts. The new default is k=10, configurable via `--neighborhood-k`.

Both fixes went in. Then it crashed the MacBook Air.

---

## The OOM

The process died at transition 14 of 20 — the 1990→2000 boundary — with exit code 137 (killed by the OS). The machine had to be restarted to recover.

The root cause was in `batch_neighborhood_shift`. The function computed a full pairwise cosine similarity matrix — every word against every other word — at each transition:

```python
sim_t1 = matrix_t1 @ matrix_t1.T  # shape (n_vocab × n_vocab)
```

For books-only decades this was fine: 20–50k words, matrices in the hundreds of megabytes. But the vocabulary sizes jump sharply once the scientific corpora enter:

```
1960s: 108,305 words
1970s: 108,633
1980s: 137,998
1990s: 157,428
2000s: 162,899
2010s: 196,308
2020s: 181,033
```

The 1990→2000 pair intersection is somewhere north of 100k words. At that size:

```
100,000 × 100,000 × 4 bytes = 40 GB per matrix
```

Two of them simultaneously — `sim_t1` and `sim_t2` — is 80 GB. The machine has 24 GB.

There were actually two layered problems. The first fix (per-pair vocab instead of global intersection) had also pre-built indices for all 21 decades simultaneously and kept them in RAM, adding another ~800 MB of pressure before the matrix multiply happened. That was fixed too — indices are now built per transition and released immediately. But the matrix multiply was the fatal one regardless.

The fix: instead of computing the full `(n_vocab × n_vocab)` similarity matrix, extract only the rows for the target words and multiply against the full vocab:

```python
# Before: n_vocab × n_vocab
sim_t1 = matrix_t1 @ matrix_t1.T

# After: n_targets × n_vocab
target_matrix_t1 = matrix_t1[[idx_t1[w] for w in chunk]]
sim_t1 = target_matrix_t1 @ matrix_t1.T
```

For 3 target words over a 100k vocabulary: `3 × 100,000 × 4 bytes = 1.2 MB`. To handle the all-words case gracefully (running without `-w` produces 5,342 target words, which at `5342 × 200k × 4 bytes ≈ 4 GB` per matrix is borderline), the processing is chunked in batches of 500 words. Peak memory per chunk: `500 × 200k × 4 bytes = 400 MB`, well within budget.

## Results

After a restart and the memory fixes, the same command finished in one second:

```
network       — 15 transitions (1880–2020)
affordance    —  5 transitions (1980–2020)
intentionality — 10 transitions (1910–2020)
```

**network**

| decade | cosine_drift | neighborhood_shift |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| 1880–1950 | ~0.00 | 0.33–0.57 |
| 1960 | 0.232 | 0.889 |
| 1970 | 0.369 | 0.947 |
| 1980 | 0.367 | 0.889 |
| 1990 | 0.436 | 0.947 |
| 2000 | 0.260 | 0.667 |
| 2010 | 0.392 | 0.889 |
| 2020 | 0.200 | 0.823 |

The signal here is almost exactly what you'd expect. Near-zero cosine drift from the 1880s through the 1950s — "network" sits stably in its philosophical and biological context across the books-only decades. Then at 1960, as PubMed and arXiv enter the corpus, both metrics spike sharply. The neighbourhood shift hitting 0.947 at 1970 and again at 1990 means the ten words most associated with "network" have almost entirely turned over — the concept has been transplanted into a different disciplinary context. The vector moved and its company changed simultaneously, which is the signature of field transfer rather than gradual drift.

**intentionality**

Present from 1910 onward. Low cosine drift through most of the 20th century (0.01–0.07), then a sustained push at 1980–2000 (cosine 0.17–0.17, neighbourhood shift 0.75–0.89). That window is when cognitive science was actively borrowing and redefining the concept — Dennett's *Intentional Stance* (1987), Searle's *Intentionality* (1983), Fodor throughout the 1980s. The metric is picking up the right decade.

**affordance**

Only five transitions, starting 1980. Low drift through 2000, then climbing at 2010–2020 (cosine 0.054 → 0.171, neighbourhood shift 0.75 → 0.889). Gibson coined the term in 1966 but it takes another two decades to propagate into the broader literature, and the recent uptick corresponds to HCI and design theory adopting it.

The `avg_pairwise_distance` column is NaN for all three — none of them appear in every decade, so the all-decades metric can't be computed. That's correct behaviour: a missing value is more honest than a misleadingly truncated one.

## Where it stands

The pipeline runs end to end without crashing. Memory usage is now bounded by target word count rather than vocabulary size — the critical fix for running on a laptop against a corpus with 200k-word decades. The Streamlit dashboard is next.

## Devlog 02 addendum — corpus expansion, retraining, and the three patterns

*March 2026*

After the first run, the corpus grew substantially. Eighteen new books were added across the decade gaps — Hegel's *Science of Logic* (1820s), Spencer's *First Principles* and *Principles of Psychology* (1850s–1860s), Bergson's *Time and Free Will* (1880s), Whewell's *History of the Inductive Sciences* (1830s), plus a long tail of 19th-century texts filling out the thin pre-1900 decades. On the contemporary end: Lukács, Lacan, Merleau-Ponty's *Visible and the Invisible*, Floridi, Ingold, Hui, Coeckelbergh, Pasquale, Crawford's *Atlas of AI*. The corpus reached 274 books across 21 decades.

The TWEC models were retrained from scratch — the compass sees the books layer, so any book addition requires a full retrain. Per-source caps were introduced at the same time: PubMed at 10,000 documents per decade file, arXiv and ACL at 5,000. Books remain uncapped. The cap matters because at 20,000 documents per file, PubMed was contributing 3–4× more training signal than arXiv and ACL combined in the 1990s–2020s.

Drift was recomputed on the new models. 592,584 rows, frequency correction fully populated.

---

### The three patterns

Running the full vocabulary drift and looking closely at five words — network, learning, agent, representation, consciousness — produced a cleaner taxonomy than the working hypothesis anticipated.

The original hypothesis was: *contested terms will show higher distributional instability than field-specific ones, and the drift analysis should show the decade where a word's pre-history gets overwritten by its computational meaning.*

That's true but incomplete. There are three distinct outcomes, not one.

**Pattern 1 — capture + stabilisation: *network*, *learning***

Both words show near-zero drift through the books-only decades, a sharp rise through the 1960s–1990s, peak instability around 1990, then a sustained drop to a low floor post-2000. The neighbourhood shift drops with it — the ten nearest neighbours stop turning over once the computational sense has won.

"Network" has the sharpest single-decade transition in the entire dataset: neighbourhood shift of 0.947 at 1960, meaning 94.7% of its top-10 neighbours were replaced in a single decade. That transition isn't the neural network revolution — it's earlier, and the 1960 neighbours tell you what it is: "arborescent, collectivity, entangles." That's Deleuze-adjacent poststructuralist vocabulary. The word entered theoretical discourse as an abstract topological concept — distributed, non-hierarchical, relational — before being adopted by computation. The 1980s ML uptake was the *second* capture, borrowing a concept that had already been theoretically abstracted. The pre-history wasn't overwritten in one step; there was a relay.

"Learning" follows the same shape but the 1960 transition is into cognitive/behaviorist psychology rather than continental theory ("kinesthetic, pavlovian, associative, experimentalist"). ML inherits the word from psychology in the 1990s. Two-step relay, same outcome: stabilised by 2000.

**Pattern 2 — permanent suspension: *agent***

"Agent" rises with the others through the 1990s but never comes back down. Drift and neighbourhood shift both stay elevated into 2020. The reason is visible in the neighbours: from 1960 through 2000, the word is dominated by biomedical vocabulary — "alkylating, antineoplastic, iron-chelating, spermicidal." PubMed's medical sense of "agent" (chemical agent, biological agent, pathogenic agent) is so voluminous that even with capping it continues to pull the word away from the AI sense. The 2020 neighbours still show both: meta-agent and dreamerv2 alongside biomedical residue.

This is structurally different from a word still drifting toward a new home. "Agent" is being held in suspension between two active, high-volume communities that both need the term and neither can monopolise it. The vector has found a stable position *between* the two clusters; what churns is the neighbourhood, as the balance shifts decade by decade. Persistent neighbourhood shift with moderate cosine drift is the signature of this state.

**Pattern 3 — continuous contestation: *representation*, *consciousness***

Neither word shows any stabilisation at any point in the 200-year record. "Consciousness" has neighbourhood shift at or near 1.000 in almost every single decade — complete turnover of its nearest neighbours, continuously, from the 1830s to the 2020s. Its 2020 neighbours are "postphenomenological, dennett, ctm, sentience" — still genuine philosophical vocabulary, still actively contested across traditions.

"Representation" has phases of relative stability (1840s–1880s) followed by explosive drift from the 1900s onward, peaking at 0.415 in 1970 and staying above 0.30 through 2010. The 2020 neighbours ("vlms, text-attributed, de-vae, hierarchy-aware") suggest ML representation learning may be beginning to capture it — but the drift floor is still far above where "network" settled.

These words are not in transit between fields. They are doing load-bearing conceptual work across multiple traditions simultaneously, and those traditions are not resolving into each other. Every decade brings a new philosophical or scientific context that reorganises what "consciousness" means and who it sits next to.

---

### What this adds to the working hypothesis

The hypothesis assumed a single overwriting event for each word. The data suggests three possible outcomes depending on the structure of the field competition:

- If one field achieves near-monopoly on usage, instability drops and the word stabilises. The prior sense is genuinely overwritten.
- If multiple high-volume fields compete with roughly equal force, the word reaches suspension — a stable geometric position that is nobody's concept in particular.
- If a word names something genuinely contested across every field that uses it — a concept without consensus — it never stabilises because there is no stable consensus to converge toward.

The instability floor after peak drift is a proxy for how thoroughly any single field has claimed the term. "Network" reached near-zero — claimed. "Agent" didn't — suspended. "Consciousness" never had a floor — permanently open.

## Where it stands

The pipeline runs end to end without crashing. Twenty-one decade models trained, drift metrics computed, results interpretable. The three-pattern taxonomy is a working framework — not a finding yet, but a structure to test against a larger word set and a more complete corpus.

Two things are visibly incomplete. The Streamlit dashboard exists but has only been run in demo mode — it needs the real aligned `.kv` files wired up and the word selector populated with the actual target vocabulary. And the corpus, while functional, is uneven: the pre-1960 decades are books-only and thin, with as few as seven books per decade in the 1830s. The models train on what's there; sparse decades produce noisier estimates. Getting the books layer denser before the next retrain is the next task.

The first results confirmed the method works. The next question is whether the signal holds as the corpus gets more complete — and whether the three patterns survive contact with the other 25 words in the target set.
