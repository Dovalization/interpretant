# Devlog 04 — The Audit, the Wrong Books, and a Corpus Built as a Grid

*November 2026*

---

Devlog 03 ended with a plan: finish sourcing books, balance the decades, then retrain. Eight months later the plan changed for two reasons.

The first was mundane. A folder reorganisation in June deleted most of the derived data: the PubMed processed files for the 1970s to the 2020s, the raw PubMed and arXiv downloads, the compass and most of the decade vectors. `drift.parquet` and the field lexicons survived, the books survived, ACL survived. Retraining was going to be a rebuild either way.

The second was the reason to rebuild differently. Before spending another training run, I wanted to know whether the corpus could answer the questions in the README at all. Not whether the pipeline runs (it does) but whether the numbers it produces can mean what devlog 02 said they meant.

---

## The audit

`corpus audit` is a new command that measures the corpus against the goals instead of against itself. It reports four things.

**Tokens per cell.** A cell is one source in one decade. The merged decade is what `embed train` actually sees.

**Target coverage.** For each word of interest, the decades where it reaches a minimum number of occurrences (150 by default), and how many consecutive decade pairs clear that bar in both decades. That second number matters more than it looks. Under TWEC, a word that appears a handful of times in a decade barely moves away from the compass vector it was initialised from. Its drift is near zero and its nearest neighbours are noise. A transition is only *reliable* if the word is well attested on both sides.

**Author concentration.** The largest author's share of each decade's book words.

**Dating risks.** Books filed outside their publication decade, and translations dated by the original instead of the English edition.

The results were worse than I expected.

- *Network* occurs between 1 and 29 times per decade from the 1820s to the 1950s. *Affordance* never reaches 150 occurrences in any decade. *Embodiment* has one reliable transition out of twenty; *intentionality* has two.
- William James was 45% of the 1890s. Kierkegaard was 30% of the 1840s, Weber 30% of the 1910s, Chalmers 31% of the 2020s. A shift between two decades was, to a large degree, a change of authors.
- The 1820s *representation* (526 occurrences) came mostly from E. F. J. Payne's 1958 translation of Schopenhauer. The 1883 Haldane–Kemp translation rendered *Vorstellung* as "Idea". The word the model learned for the 1820s was a 1950s translator's choice.
- The 1820s to 1950s were books only. PubMed entered in the 1960s with 13 million tokens against 3 million of books.

A fifth problem came out later, in the training code rather than the corpus. gensim's compiled training loop silently stops reading a sentence after 10,000 tokens, and the books are stored one book per line, about 120,000 tokens each. Every model trained so far saw the first 10,000 tokens of each book — title pages, prefaces, tables of contents — and nothing after. More than nine tenths of the books layer never reached the embeddings. The fix is a few lines: split each line into 10,000-token sentences, which loses only the handful of context windows that cross a split.

The PubMed point is the uncomfortable one. Devlog 02 reported that *network*'s neighbourhood "completely turns over at 1960". It does, but 1960 is also exactly where the corpus stops being nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century books and becomes mostly biomedical abstracts. And before 1960 *network* was too rare for its vector to mean much. The turnover is real in the data and uninterpretable as a claim about the concept. The same caution applies to the *intentionality* spike in the 1980s (a word with two reliable transitions, in a decade that contains Searle's *Intentionality*) and to *affordance* "starting to move" in 2010 (a word that never cleared the threshold anywhere).

I'm not retracting devlog 02. The pipeline findings in it, the training fixes and the OOM, stand. But its three drift patterns (capture and stabilisation, permanent suspension, continuous contestation) are on hold until they survive a corpus that can carry them.

## Corpus archaeology, again

The dating check led somewhere I didn't expect. To re-date the translations, I opened every flagged file to find which edition it actually contained. Nineteen weren't usable. Seventeen were a different book entirely; Frege was the German original, and Morris's *Signs, Language and Behavior* had a font encoding that extracted as noise.

Four more were the right work in a different translation from the one the manifest claimed: Hegel's *Phenomenology* was Michael Inwood's 2018 translation, not Baillie's; Kant was Meiklejohn's 1855, not Kemp Smith's; Descartes was the *Discourse*, not the *Meditations*. And a dozen files used a legacy `file` key that the stats code silently counted as present because their decade folder existed.

The lesson is the same one devlog 03 drew, one level down: corpus decisions are modelling decisions, and a manifest is a claim about the corpus, not a description of it. The fix was to stop trusting it:

- `scripts/redate_books.py` records every edition decision, with the evidence from each file's own front matter, and applies them. Every translation now carries `translator`, `original_language` and `original_year`; `year` is the English edition in the file; the file lives in that decade's folder. A translation is a different community's use of the sign — which, for a project named after Peirce's interpretant, is the point.
- Fifteen wrong files were replaced with the right public-domain edition, and three more by hand: Morris's *Signs, Language and Behavior* (OCR of the 1946 edition; the old file had a broken font), Lowrie's *Concept of Dread* and Austin's translation of Frege. Benjamin's *Illuminations* turned out to contain only one essay; the complete Zohn translation replaced it.
- Pre-1820 English originals (Coleridge, *Frankenstein*) moved to a `canon/` folder outside the decade grid.

After the fixes the audit reports no dating risks, and the books grew from 31.9 to 42.3 million tokens. The rare-word problem barely moved: re-dating cannot create occurrences. That is a volume problem, and it needs a different kind of corpus.

---

## A corpus built as a grid

Every goal in the README compares something specific: one field across decades, or several fields within a decade. The old corpus merged everything into one model per decade, so field trajectories, borrowing and divergence were never measured — they were blended.

The new design is a grid: one cell per field and decade, each built the same way. The scientific layer comes from OpenAlex, which classifies every work with a single topic taxonomy, so philosophy, psychology, neuroscience, computer science, linguistics and the social sciences share labels across the whole series. `corpus openalex download` takes a seeded random sample of up to 10,000 English abstracts per cell for eleven fields, from the 1950s to the 2020s, and keeps each work's author ids so an author cap can be applied later.

A probe before the download set expectations. Most fields have enough abstracts from the 1980s; HCI and communication from the 1990s; only a few fields work in the 1950s and 60s. The probe also taught a small lesson about tools: OpenAlex's abstract search matches word stems, so its counts for *affordance* included *afford* and *affordable* — it found 19 philosophy abstracts mentioning affordance in the 1950s, a decade before Gibson coined the word. The real count, once the abstracts were downloaded, was zero.

The first download also showed how much of OpenAlex is not research prose. Albert Sabin's correspondence sat in the 1950s history and philosophy of science as hundreds of "Letter from Sabin, Albert B. to ..." records; a quarter of the 1970s communication sample was news scripts from one Fort Worth TV station, all with the same "abstract"; other abstracts were scraped publisher pages (citation-export menus, share bars, paywall banners), citation strings, or text in Finnish, Polish and Spanish labelled as English. The final cells keep research prose only (articles, reviews, book chapters, books, dissertations, conference papers), and the ingest drops duplicates, catalogue records, scraped pages and non-English text and caps any author at 1% of a cell. Of about 730,000 downloaded works, 677,000 remain.

Where a word is too rare for a 10,000-abstract cell, the home field was downloaded in full: HCI for the 2000s–2020s and philosophy for the 1980s–2020s. These cells serve within-field trajectories only; comparing a 200,000-abstract cell with a 10,000-abstract one would mix size with meaning.

The counts alone already showed things the merged corpus could not:

- *Affordance* barely exists outside HCI. In HCI's full cells it grows from 503 occurrences in the 2000s to 1,930 in the 2020s; in philosophy it appears only in the 2010s (159) and 2020s (294).
- *Embodiment* grows twelvefold in HCI (222 to 2,809 occurrences) and is now about three times as frequent there as in philosophy (202 against 66 per million tokens).
- *Consciousness* splits in the 2020s: psychology's use falls by two thirds (149 to 52 per 10,000 abstracts) while philosophy's rises by 60% (1,049 to 1,712). A second, independently seeded sample gives almost the same counts (1,723 and 55).
- *Information* is the one target frequent in every field from the 1950s on, and in linguistics *interface* jumps in the 1990s: the syntax–semantics interface of Minimalist syntax, a sense a merged model would average away.

## Fixing the questions before the answers

One more change, made before any retraining so it can't be tuned to results: the target list is now fixed and tracked. It has three tiers — a core measurable across most of the grid, late arrivals whose series begin where they first clear the threshold (*affordance*, *embodiment*, *intentionality*, *interface*, *user*, *agent*, *simulation*), and two context-only words read in the books and communication cells. *Cognition*, *computation*, *emergence*, *meaning*, *sign*, *system* and *control* joined; *understanding*, *form*, *pattern* and *signal* left, because their everyday senses outweigh any conceptual change.

Preprocessing now maps plurals and British spellings of the targets onto one lemma, so *representations* and *behaviour* stop splitting their words into separate vectors. And "a lot of drift" needs a reference. The first plan was a set of everyday control words, but everyday words are rare in abstracts, too rare in most cells to carry a noise floor. So the floor comes from the corpus itself, every word measured in a pair of cells, and the control words only check it.

## Training, and testing the answers

Each cell got its own model: 117 TWEC models (21 books decades, 88 OpenAlex cells, 8 full cells), all trained against one compass built from the same token budget from every cell, so the large recent cells do not define the space for the 1850s. The settings changed for the smaller cells: 100 dimensions, a 5-word window, 10 epochs, 5 negative samples. A full run takes about 40 minutes on the laptop.

Drift is judged against the corpus itself. For each pair of cells, every word that occurs at least 150 times in both is ranked within its frequency bin on two measures, how far its vector moved and how many of its 25 nearest neighbours changed; a word is a *candidate* only in the top 5% on both. Everyday control words (*people*, *day*, *water*, ...) check the floor: they were flagged in under 1% of measurements. The exception is *food*, flagged in 4 of the 27 field pairs where it is measured, more than chance allows: psychology, sociology and neuroscience genuinely mean different things by it, and it broke the limit I had committed to in advance, twice.

Before running any of it, I wrote down what I expected and committed it to git, timestamped, so the predictions could not drift towards the results. Then three rounds, each tested on evidence that did not exist when it was written:

- **Round 1** (the trained models): 3 of 9 predictions passed. *Representation* in AI moved from symbolic (*knowledge, declarative, frame-based*) to learned (*embeddings, latent, contrastive*), and *intentionality* stayed put. The interesting part was the failures: I predicted HCI's *embodiment* would sit near *tangible* and *interaction*; it sits near *presence, ownership, illusion, self-location* — the sense of embodiment in virtual reality.
- **Round 2** (a second training seed, plus decades and field pairs round 1 never looked at): 7 of 9 passed, including the surprises from round 1, now stated as predictions. Every candidate also changed more between its cells than between two trainings of the same cell — which matters, because retraining moves an ordinary word's neighbours by about 0.4 on a 0–1 scale, two thirds of its typical change between decades.
- **Round 3** (a second method, count-based PPMI-SVD): 4 of 8 passed. The methods agree in general, and they agree on *embodiment* and *intentionality*; they disagree on *information* and only half agree on *consciousness*.

What survives all of it:

- ***Embodiment* has two meanings.** In HCI it is virtual reality's sense of body ownership and presence; in philosophy it is phenomenological corporeality (*intersubjectivity, temporality, spatiality, being-in-the-world*). It passed every test it faced: unseen decades, a second seed, a second method. The same word, borrowed into a new field, does a different job there.
- ***Intentionality* is stable** in philosophy across five decades, under both methods.

What does not, or not yet: *information* converging between psychology and computer science shows up in both TWEC runs and not in PPMI-SVD; philosophy's 2020s *consciousness* changes under both methods, but TWEC describes the change with computational vocabulary and PPMI-SVD with named theories of consciousness (*orch-or, workspace, iit*). Both stay open.

My guesses before each round were worse than the method: I called several round 2 passes coin flips. The design — large cells, two measures, a corpus-wide floor — turned out more conservative than I expected, and the predictions file records every miss.

---

## Where it stands

The corpus is rebuilt, trained twice and checked with a second method. Next:

- cross-perplexity between cells, to measure who borrows from whom;
- a held-out check per cell and a backoff rule for sparse cells;
- the dashboard, pointed at the new drift tables;
- and, for the open findings, sense-level drift with contextual embeddings, where *consciousness* and *information* can be split into their senses instead of averaged.

The devlog 02 patterns are not back; two findings are, and they are better supported than anything the old corpus produced.
