# Devlog 08 — Senses Written Down, and a Test That Failed in the Right Places

*November 2026*

---

Devlog 07 ended with a rule for any new instrument: a few known answers and a stopping rule,
committed before it says anything. The next instrument after the grid compared pairs of
occurrences of a word with a contextual encoder, and it found every documented change the grid
had missed. It also found a transition where nothing documented happened, and a placebo every
time I planted one, though that placebo turned out to change the sense mix as well. Reading its pairs by hand showed why. The measure answered "did the contexts
change?", not "did the meaning change?", and in this corpus the contexts of almost every word
change between decades.

So I turned the question round. Instead of asking how similar two occurrences are, ask which
sense each one has, from a short list written for that word, and read a cell's meaning as its
share of each sense. A change of context with no change of sense should then leave the shares
where they were.

## Writing the senses down

Each word got two to six senses, each a one-line definition and an invented example, and for
each sense a handful of cue words that signal it when they appear near the word: *transformer*,
*softmax* or *encoder* for the attention mechanism, *ChatGPT* or *engineering* for a
language model's prompt. Some cues silence a sense instead (*platform*'s physical structure stops
counting when *tv* is nearby, because a satellite TV platform is a service). Some words put a
usage out of scope altogether: the terms of grammatical alignment, *ergative*, *accusative* and
the rest, so linguistics' use of *alignment* is neither trained on nor counted. And a sentence
that turns up in five or more usages of the same word is not an abstract's own text but a
publisher's notice or a series line, and it is dropped.

Claude drafted the lists and cues. I did not label examples. The original plan had me labelling
about 130 usages for the second test, and I had already found the 100 pair judgements of the
earlier check slow enough to dread another round of them. What I did instead was review: a page
per word showing each sense's definition, its cues, the sentences those cues caught across the
whole corpus, and two checks that flagged the doubtful ones, a cue whose sentences a model
trained without them disagreed with, and a sentence a model that never saw it would label
differently. I approved, narrowed, merged and split. That is 46 commits marked "after the
author's review": cues held to the word right before the target, *communication* dropped from
computer networks, planning split into general and urban planning, AI alignment merged into a
broader goal-or-value alignment because, with 33 training sentences and more than half of them
doubtful, the classifier could not tell it apart. Then the lists were frozen and committed
before anything they labelled was scored.

## A classifier, not a language model

The labeller is deliberately plain. Every usage whose cues name exactly one sense becomes a
training sentence; XL-LEXEME, an encoder trained on whether two occurrences share a meaning,
turns each into a vector; and a logistic regression per word learns the senses from those
vectors and labels every usage, cue or no cue. A cell's shares are then corrected for the
classifier's known confusions.

I could have asked a language model to label everything. Two of them, Claude Sonnet and a
decision model, JEV, label the same usages as comparison points, and on a human-judged benchmark
of 46 English words, before this corpus, the classifier ranked the changes almost as well as
Sonnet did. But this
is an NLP project, and I want the method to be one I can take apart: cue rules as weak
supervision, a linear probe on frozen embeddings, a correction anyone can recompute. A language
model is a measurement to compare against, not the default.

## Round T: short by a little

The first test on this corpus used the pairs I had already judged: 100 pairs of usages of
*attention, prompt, platform, network, component* and *having*. Two predictions, both falsified.
The overlap of the classifier's sense probabilities agreed with my judgements at 0.449, against a
bar of 0.456, the encoder's raw similarity on the same pairs. Its "same sense or not" agreed at
0.397, against 0.414, Sonnet's 0.514 less a margin of 0.10. Close on both, and both misses.

Reading the misses pair by pair, three things stood out. Some senses were thin: *prompt* had 273
training sentences against 6,713 for *attention*, and it was the word where the classifier fell
furthest behind (0.46 against Sonnet's 0.75, on 16 pairs). Balanced class weights, which make a
small sense count as much as a large one, pulled uncertain usages towards the small senses. And
some cues were simply missing.

The plan said no repair rounds. I made one exception, and wrote it into the predictions file
before doing anything: a revised classifier would get one new round, on fresh sentences, never
on round T's pairs, and if it failed the language model would label instead. The revision
softened the weights to square-root balance, let a usage whose cues name exactly one sense take
that sense outright, added cues to six senses, and lifted the sampling cap for *prompt* in the
two cells where it bit. It was tuned on 300 development usages labelled blind before any
classifier output was seen, where it went from 234 to 252 agreeing. Those figures are optimistic
by construction, and they were not the test.

## The head-to-head round

The test was 240 new usages, 40 per word. The classifier and Sonnet labelled them all, and
disagreed on 48. Each of those came to me on a page with the two labels' definitions in random
order, nothing saying which labeller gave which. I clicked through all 48.

Of the 41 where I chose exactly one, the classifier's label won 18: 43.9%, against a bar of 40%.
Both fitted three times and neither fitted four. It passed, narrowly; 41 decisions leave an
interval of roughly 30% to 60% around that figure. And it lost seven of eight on *prompt*. Two of
those were 1980s interface prompts, "menus and prompts", read as a language model's input.

## Round A

Round A gave the classifier the known changes again: *attention* in AI from the 2010s to the
2020s, *prompt* in AI from the 2000s to the 2020s, *platform* in communication from the 2000s to
the 2010s, *network* in AI from the 1980s to the 1990s. Two quiet transitions where nothing
documented happened. A change counts as found only if it beats both a shuffle of the word's own
usages between the two cells and the 90th percentile of ten control words on the same
transition. Then a planted change, psychology's 2020s *attention* with a growing share swapped
for AI's attention-mechanism usages, and a placebo built so that it should not register:
psychology's 2020s *years* with 40% swapped for sociology's, a change of source with the same
senses.

Before the run I added the classifier from the head-to-head round to the plan, and wrote down
four ways it could go wrong. The first was *prompt*: the dialogue-system prompts of AI's 2000s
read as language-model input would raise the earlier share and shrink the rise.

A1 failed: the classifier found two of the four documented changes, *attention* and *platform*.
*prompt* and *network* beat their shuffles but not their controls. Everything else passed. Neither
quiet transition was found. In all four documented changes the documented sense rose, the
attention mechanism from 0.175 to 0.518 of AI's *attention*, the artificial neural network from
0.610 to 0.811 of its *network*. The planted change was found in three of five draws at 10% and
in every draw from 20% up. The placebo was found in none of five.

Those last two are where the usage-pair measure fell short. It needed a third of the usages
planted before it saw a change, and its placebo registered every time, though that placebo also
carried some mechanism uses. This one held the senses fixed: the labels see a fifth, and a change
of source alone never registered.

The *prompt* miss is the risk I had written down. The classifier labelled 66 of AI's 150 2000s
*prompt* usages as language-model input; Sonnet labelled 5 of all 150 that way. Read, they are
spoken-dialogue systems prompting for information, and an ontology tool called PROMPT. That
inflates the earlier share and shrinks the change below the controls' bar. *network* I can only
read as exploration: the connectionist turn happened inside the 1980s, and a 1980s cell already
holds it, so a decade bin blurs the very change it is meant to show.

## What it taught

**A spent test is development data.** Round T's pairs, once scored, could not judge the revised
classifier, so the second round drew new sentences and kept them unread, and out of training, until
I had judged them. Round A's cells are spent the same way now.

**The controls are the hard part.** Under the shuffle bar alone the classifier matches the
language models: Sonnet and JEV each find all four documented changes and neither quiet
transition, and so does the classifier. It fails only on the controls' bar, and the language
models never faced it, because they did not label the control words. One in five control-word
transitions beat its own shuffles, which is why that bar exists.

**Granularity is a decision, and every decision shows up later.** AI alignment folded into a
broader sense because it could not stand alone. *network*'s vascular, gene and citation networks
fit none of its senses and are forced into one. *prompt* is a noun, a verb and an adjective, and
cues written for one fire on another. Decades are a granularity too.

## Where it stands

The robust finding is unchanged: *embodiment*'s two senses. The sense labels failed round A on
A1, so by the rule this version stops before open questions. It passed the placebo and the
planted change, the two tests aimed at the context-for-meaning confusion that stopped the
instrument before it, and it fell short on two documented changes for reasons I had written down
in advance or can see in the cells. Next:

- the corpus is cleaner already: the scan below surfaced abstracts in other languages, student
  newspapers indexed as research and exam-dump spam, and a second set of quality rules now drops
  them (and strips publishers' notice sentences) from every round from here on;
- improve the classifier on development data only, round A's cells included;
- a fresh set of known answers, *hallucination*, *transformer*, *cloud* and *embedding*, at
  half-decade windows instead of decades. The test set is already written down and committed,
  with a source for each date, before any of its data was downloaded; the predictions and a
  stopping rule follow before it runs;
- encode the whole grid once on the home GPU;
- a corpus-wide scan for candidates has already surfaced words such as *presence*, *strength*,
  *fusion* and *agency*. They are candidates, and they wait until an instrument has passed.
