# Devlog 03 — Field Dynamics, Corpus Archaeology, and 49 Books from the Public Domain

*March 2026*

---

Devlog 02 closed with three patterns: capture and stabilisation, permanent suspension, continuous contestation. The pipeline ran end to end, the metrics looked right, and the three-pattern taxonomy gave the results a coherent shape.

That was the word-level story. The question that opened next was whether you could see the same dynamics at the level of fields — not *how does "network" drift*, but *which fields are gaining and losing territory over time, and can you read the field competition directly off the corpus structure?*

---

## A second visualization layer

The drift metrics produce a per-word time series. What they don't produce is a picture of the whole semantic neighbourhood — the community of terms around each target word, and which disciplines those communities belong to.

To get that, I built a separate analysis layer in `scripts/plot_fields.py`. The logic: assign each word in the shared vocabulary to one of six display fields — ML/deep learning, Neuroscience/biology, Philosophy of mind, Cognitive science, Cybernetics/systems, Social theory — using two methods in combination.

The first method is lexicon lookup. The field lexicons are extracted from the books corpus using TF-IDF: for each field, average the TF-IDF scores across all books tagged with that field's manifest labels, and take the top-60 terms by mean score. A word that appears in a field's lexicon is attributed to that field directly.

The second method is centroid proximity. For words that don't appear in any lexicon, compute their cosine similarity to each field's centroid vector (the mean of all vectors for that field's lexicon words in a given decade) and assign to the nearest field. This is the fallback; lexicon attribution takes precedence.

The combination gives clean attribution for field-core vocabulary and principled interpolation for the ambiguous middle.

From that, four plots:

**Neighbour matrix** — for each of the five target words across all 21 decades, visualise the top-20 nearest neighbours as a coloured dot matrix, one colour per field. The matrix makes field transitions readable at a glance: "network" is a river of social-theory blue running through the 19th-century decades, with ML orange flooding in from the right.

**Community fingerprint** — a radar/rose chart per target word per decade, showing the field composition of its neighbourhood as proportions. Useful for seeing whether a word is cleanly associated with one field (spoke-dominant) or genuinely split across several (circular).

**Churn rate** — which terms entered and exited each word's neighbourhood each decade, broken down by field. If social theory words are leaving a neighbourhood while ML words are entering, the churn bars make the direction of travel visible.

**Influence graph** — a bipartite layout with target words across the top and "bridge concepts" below — terms that appear in multiple target words' neighbourhoods simultaneously. These are the conceptual connectives, the terms that hold the vocabulary of different disciplines in contact with each other.

**Field composition timeline** — a stacked area chart per target word showing how the field composition of its neighbourhood evolves over 200 years. The horizontal axis is time; the stacked areas show how much of the neighbourhood belongs to each field at each decade.

---

## What the plots revealed

The neighbour matrix was the most immediately legible. Five words, 21 decades, coloured by field — you can read field takeover events directly as colour transitions.

"Network" shows what we expected: a near-solid run of philosophy-of-mind and social theory attribution through the 19th century, a gradual introduction of cybernetics/systems vocabulary from the 1940s–1960s, then ML orange crowding in from the 1990s onward. The 1960 neighbourhood shift (0.947 in devlog 02) corresponds to the social-theory-to-cybernetics transition — what looked like sudden drift at the word level reads as a field handoff in the neighbourhood matrix.

"Consciousness" is the opposite of legible. The dots stay mixed across all 21 decades — no single field ever achieves neighbourhood dominance. Philosophy of mind is always present but never hegemonic. Cognitive science enters the neighbourhood in the 1960s but doesn't displace the earlier terms; it adds to a field composition that was already contested. The community fingerprint for "consciousness" looks like a clock face at every decade — spokes in all directions, nothing dominant.

"Affordance" tells a cleaner story. Pre-Gibson, the word barely appears. Post-1970, its neighbourhood is cleanly cognitive science and philosophy of mind. The recent drift (2010–2020) shows HCI and design vocabulary entering the neighbourhood — "user", "interface", "interaction" — which is the affordance-concept being operationalised by a design community that inherited it from cognitive science.

The churn plots showed something the drift metrics alone couldn't: the directionality within field turnover. When cybernetics/systems terms enter "network"'s neighbourhood in the 1950s–1960s, social theory terms are simultaneously exiting. The field handoff isn't accumulation — it's replacement. By contrast, "agent"'s churn shows biomedical terms entering *without* proportional exit from any other field, which is consistent with the "permanent suspension" reading: PubMed is adding mass to the word's neighbourhood without displacing its existing associations.

The influence graph was the most surprising result. The bridge concepts — terms that appear in multiple target words' neighbourhoods simultaneously — are almost entirely from the cybernetics/systems field. "Information", "feedback", "control", "signal", "communication" are the connectives that hold the vocabulary of AI, cognitive science, and neuroscience in contact with each other. Philosophy of mind vocabulary almost never bridges. Philosophical concepts tend to be locally dense and inter-referential but rarely appear in the neighbourhoods of words from adjacent fields. The fields talk through cybernetics rather than directly.

## The corpus quality problem

The field lexicon extraction produced a result that stopped me immediately.

The "Neuroscience / biology" lexicons were dominated by Freudian vocabulary: *libido*, *neurosis*, *oedipus*, *fixation*, *hysteria*. That's not a neuroscience lexicon — it's a psychoanalysis lexicon, and it was appearing because the manifest field label `psychoanalysis` had been grouped with `biology` in the FIELD_MAP. Freud's books in the corpus (four of them — *Interpretation of Dreams*, *Psychopathology of Everyday Life*, *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*, *The Ego and the Id*) were contributing most of the TF-IDF signal that was supposed to represent neuroscience.

The fix required changes at two levels. In `extract_field_lexicons.py`, the field map was corrected — `psychoanalysis` removed from the neuroscience group, replaced with a new `neuroscience` manifest tag. In the manifest, eight books received the new tag: Sherrington's *Integrative Action of the Nervous System*, Pavlov's *Conditioned Reflexes*, Hebb's *The Organization of Behavior*, Dayan and Abbott's *Theoretical Neuroscience*, and four existing books that had been tagged ambiguously — Descartes' *Meditations*, Helmholtz's *Physiological Optics*, Wundt's *Principles of Physiological Psychology*, and Marr's *Vision*.

Regenerating the lexicons after this change produced what the field should have looked like: *synapse*, *cortex*, *reflex*, *inhibitory*, *receptor*, *excitatory*, *axon*, *hippocampus*.

That one correction revealed a deeper problem with the corpus structure.

Only four decades meet a threshold of 20 books — and those four happen to be the decades where scientific corpora (PubMed, arXiv, ACL) are also present and providing the bulk of training signal. In the decades where the books layer is doing all the work, the corpus is thin. Seven books for the 1830s means seven documents to establish the philosophical vocabulary for an entire decade. If a term doesn't appear in those seven texts, it simply doesn't exist in the 1830s model — not because the concept was absent from 19th century thought, but because the corpus didn't include the right books.

This is a structural bias, not a sampling error. A word that was central to 1840s Kierkegaard — *subjectivity*, *anxiety*, *repetition*, *leap* — doesn't appear in the 1840s model if Kierkegaard isn't in the corpus. The drift signal for those words starts effectively at the decade where they enter through Heidegger or the Frankfurt School. The pre-history is invisible, and the model treats it as absence rather than gap.

The target: 20 books minimum per decade.

## Corpus archaeology

The first audit was of the manifest itself. Of the 240 books listed in devlog 01, 99 had status `pending` — meaning the entries existed but the text files were supposedly missing. Checking the actual decade directories against the manifest revealed that all 99 texts were already on disk. The pipeline reads everything in the decade folders directly — it doesn't consult manifest status — so those books had been part of every training run. The manifest was stale bookkeeping, not a real gap.

After updating all 99 to `available`, the actual count was 240 books fully in the corpus, not the partial set the manifest implied.

That still left the decade imbalance. The approach was two-pronged: automated fetch from open-access sources, and a manual sourcing list for everything that requires tracking down a PDF.

---

## 49 books from the public domain

Project Gutenberg and the Internet Archive together cover most of the 19th-century gaps and a surprising amount of the early 20th century. The public domain cutoff (pre-1928 in the US) means that essentially everything through the 1910s is freely available in plain text.

I built a fetch script — `scripts/fetch_open_access.py` — that queries Gutenberg's plain-text cache and Archive.org's OCR text layer, strips boilerplate, and places the extracted text in the appropriate decade folder, adding each entry to the manifest. 57 titles attempted; 49 fetched successfully. Eight failed — four with 403/404 errors (access-restricted or simply not digitised), four with 503 rate-limit errors that should resolve on retry.

What those 49 books add:

**19th century fills**: Thomas Brown's *Lectures on the Philosophy of the Human Mind* (1820s, the clearest pre-Freudian treatment of association and mental chemistry), Harriet Martineau's *Society in America* (1830s, foundational observational sociology), Hippolyte Taine's *On Intelligence* (1870s, an early attempt at a naturalistic account of mental representation), Ernst Mach's *Analysis of Sensations* (1880s, the direct ancestor of Gestalt psychology and of Wittgenstein's early philosophy), Karl Pearson's *Grammar of Science* (1890s, the philosophical statement of statistical empiricism before it became statistics).

**Unexpected finds**: Watson's *Behaviorism* (1924) was on Archive.org. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* and Mannheim's *Ideology and Utopia* were listed as available but returned 503 errors — rate-limited, likely retryable. Walter Benjamin's *Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* was present as a freely uploaded PDF (short text, 10,690 words — useful signal but thin). Cassirer's *Essay on Man* (1944) was available via a Digital Library of India scan.

**What's still needed**: the continental European tradition is largely undigitised in English translation — Kierkegaard, Durkheim, Simmel, Tönnies, Dilthey, Sartre, Mannheim, Husserl. These are the most methodologically important gaps: they represent the social theory and phenomenology lineage that feeds directly into the vocabulary of interpretation, intersubjectivity, lifeworld, and structure that circulates through the human sciences from the 1960s onward. They'll require manual sourcing.

Most pre-1960 decades are now at 12–18 books and approaching the 20-book threshold. The worst remaining gaps — 1830s at 12, 1890s at 12, 1930s at 11, 2020s at 5 — require the manual sourcing list: Durkheim, Simmel, Tönnies, Ayer, Mannheim, Husserl, Popper, Craik, Chomsky, and a cluster of recent AI-critical texts that aren't yet in the public domain.

The pipeline will be retrained once that sourcing is complete. A partial retrain now would produce results biased toward whichever fields happen to have been filled — better to get the corpus as balanced as possible before committing another full training run.

The field visualization layer, the decade balance target, and the structural bias audit are all evidence of the same underlying problem: corpus decisions are modeling decisions. The TWEC architecture guarantees that decade models share a coordinate system; it doesn't guarantee that the coordinate system is being estimated from representative data. Getting that right is prior to any analysis of what the results mean.
