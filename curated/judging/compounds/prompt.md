You are checking a compound map for a corpus of English research abstracts (1950s to 2020s; fields
such as artificial intelligence, human-computer interaction, computer science, cognitive
psychology, linguistics, communication, sociology, philosophy and history of science). A two-part
compound can be written open ("data base"), hyphenated ("data-base") or closed ("database"). Each
item is a pair: FORM will be written as STANDARD in the corpus's word lists, on the ground that the
two are the same compound written another way. Which of the two is the standard was set by a rule
(the form the corpus uses most often); do not judge the direction. An open FORM is only ever two
nouns side by side.

Judge each pair at the level of the word, not of the two sentences:

- right: FORM and STANDARD are the same word or compound, written open, hyphenated or closed.
  Different senses of it in the sentences do not make a pair wrong, because every way of writing
  it carries all of its senses. A stray hyphen left by a line break counts as right ("meth-od" is
  "method"; "in-formation" is "information").
- wrong: the way of writing marks a different word ("re-cover" against "recover", "re-form"
  against "reform", "re-sign" against "resign", "re-creation" against "recreation"); the open form
  is two words that happen to stand together rather than the compound (as in "green house" for a
  house painted green against "greenhouse"); either form is a letter code, an abbreviation or a
  chemical or gene name; or either form is not English.

Names and other languages are handled separately. If a sentence shows a form only as a name or
inside another language, judge the English word the pair stands for.

Each item gives the pair and up to two sentences from the corpus for FORM and for STANDARD, with
the occurrence marked. Use them to see how the corpus uses each form, together with your knowledge
of English; do not look anything up and do not run code.
